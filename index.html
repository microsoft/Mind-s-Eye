Mind's Eye

A Benchmark of Visual Abstraction, Transformation and Composition for Multimodal LLMs

Rohit Sinha, Aditya Kanade, Sai Srinivas Kancheti, Vineeth N Balasubramanian, Tanuja Ganu

Microsoft Research & IIT Hyderabad

📄 Paper 🖋 arXiv 💻 Code

Overview

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have achieved impressive progress on vision language benchmarks, yet their capacity for visual cognitive and visuospatial reasoning remains less understood. We introduce Mind's Eye, a multiple-choice benchmark of eight visuo-cognitive tasks inspired by classic human intelligence tests and organized under a novel A–R–T taxonomy: Abstraction, Relation, and Transformation.

The tasks probe core processes of fluid intelligence such as pattern induction, analogical relation mapping, and mental transformation. We evaluate a diverse suite of closed-source and open-source MLLMs and compare their performance with human participants. Humans achieve 80% accuracy, while top performing MLLMs remain below 50%. Our findings suggest that current MLLMs exhibit limited visuospatial reasoning capabilities, highlighting the need for more cognitively grounded evaluation frameworks.

Key Insights

Mind's Eye organizes visuocognitive reasoning along three complementary dimensions, drawing on Carroll's Three-Stratum Theory of fluid intelligence:

  • Abstraction requires inducing latent structure from surface variation—identifying underlying rules, patterns, or categories that unify disparate visual instances.
  • Relation requires mapping correspondences across visual structures—detecting how elements in one configuration align with elements in another, supporting analogical transfer.
  • Transformation requires mentally simulating spatial operations—internally rotating, folding, composing, or otherwise manipulating visual representations to predict outcomes.

This framework makes explicit the component processes of visuospatial intelligence, enabling targeted diagnosis of where models succeed or fail.

Mind's Eye comprises eight parametrically controlled tasks distributed across the ART dimensions, each operationalized as a multiple-choice problem with diagnostic distractors:

  • Visual Relation Abstraction (VRA): Inducing latent rules from visual exemplars.
  • Hierarchical Pattern Equivalence (HPE): Detecting recursive structure across configurations.
  • Dynamic Structural Correspondence (DSC): Tracking unidirectional changes across sequences.
  • Visual Conceptual Slippage (VCS): Detecting violations of relational invariants.
  • Symmetric Structures (SS): Mapping structural correspondences and detecting symmetries.
  • Mental Transformation (MT): Mentally rotating 3D objects, adapted from classic cognitive tests.
  • Paper Folding (PF): Predicting outcomes of folding and unfolding operations.
  • Mental Composition (MC): Mentally folding nets into 3D shapes.

All stimuli are programmatically generated as scalable vector graphics, ensuring perceptual uniformity and enabling fine-grained control over task difficulty. The evaluation set comprises 800 items (100 per task), with an extended set of 20,000 items available for training.

Results: A Stark Human–Model Gap

Our evaluation of 18 MLLMs reveals significant underperformance relative to humans in visuospatial reasoning:

  • Non-expert humans achieve an average accuracy of 80% across all tasks.
  • The best-performing MLLMs remain below 50%, with most open-source models near 30%.
  • No single model consistently excels across all cognitive dimensions, indicating scattered and unreliable capabilities.

Abstraction

Models struggle with tasks requiring pattern induction and generalization. Gemini-2.5 achieves the best VRA score at 29.0%, far below human performance of 68%.

Relation

Relation tasks test analogical mapping. VCS proves particularly challenging, with the best model (Gemini-2.5) reaching only 35.3% vs. human 87%.

Transformation

Mental Composition shows the strongest model performance, with Gemini-2.5 at 51.8%, yet Mental Transformation and Paper Folding remain well below human accuracy of ~80%.

A striking finding is that MLLM performance remains flat across difficulty levels, while human accuracy degrades predictably:

  • Human accuracy drops from >80% on Easy items to ~25% on Hard items, reflecting genuine cognitive challenge.
  • Both open and closed-source models exhibit flat performance curves (20–45%) regardless of difficulty.
  • This difficulty-invariant failure suggests MLLMs lack foundational visuo-cognitive operations rather than merely struggling with complexity.

Humans fail because the task is hard; models fail because they cannot perform the underlying operation at any difficulty level.

Analysis: Why Do MLLMs Fail?

Our diagnostic analyses reveal several fundamental limitations in current MLLM architectures that explain the persistent human–model gap.

We analyzed whether option-directed attention predicts reasoning success. Key findings:

  • Option-Specific Attention Score correlates positively with accuracy (point-biserial r = 0.34, p < 0.001).
  • Yet even in the highest-attention quartile, accuracy remains well below human performance (>80%).
  • On correct predictions, attention to the correct option exceeds attention to distractors (0.24 vs. 0.16). On incorrect predictions, no such preference exists.

Models can identify where to look but not how to reason over what they see—attention alignment is necessary but not sufficient.

Prompting strategies (CoT, Meta-task, Step-by-step, Hints) yield dimension-dependent rather than uniformly beneficial effects:

  • Abstraction tasks benefit from structured guidance, with Meta-task and Step-by-step prompting yielding gains of approximately +1.3 pts.
  • Transformation tasks exhibit consistent performance degradation across all alternative prompting strategies, with Hint prompting showing the largest drop (~−0.9 pts).
  • Relation tasks show intermediate behavior, with modest improvements under Meta-task prompting.

Scaffolding facilitates rule derivation yet fails to elicit procedural visuocognitive operations required for transformation reasoning.

Analysis of model behavior across scales and task types reveals:

  • Performance generally increases with model size, reinforcing the role of parameter scale.
  • However, models show strong dependence on surface perception: in Mental Composition, models succeed when unfolded nets visually resemble a cube but fail when correct inference requires mental folding.
  • Models struggle to cognitively group figures and concepts into coherent representations, frequently selecting implausible answers.

Scaling tends to improve surface-matching tasks more than those requiring internal simulation.

Conclusion

Mind's Eye reveals a persistent human–model gap on visuo-cognitive tasks: non-expert humans achieve 80% mean accuracy, whereas top MLLMs stay below 50%. Key takeaways include: (i) MLLMs rely heavily on perceptual cues with limited coupling between textual reasoning and visual evidence; (ii) prompting strategies yield modest improvements but do not introduce new visuo-cognitive capabilities; (iii) scaling improves surface-matching tasks more than those requiring internal simulation.

Our ART-aligned, parametric design exposes where failures occur, providing directions for modeling advances in grounded attention, spatial working memory, and transformation-aware representations. Future directions include open-ended answer choices, 3D perception tasks, and human studies across varying expertise levels.
